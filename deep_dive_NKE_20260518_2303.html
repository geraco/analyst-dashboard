## Executive Summary

Nike is a globally iconic brand in severe operational and strategic distress. The stock is trading near 12-year lows, down ~45% from its 52-week high, and has suffered a -29.7% one-year return. The once-unstoppable growth engine has stalled: revenue is essentially flat, earnings have collapsed (-34.8% YoY), and margins have compressed dramatically. The China market — once Nike's crown jewel growth vector — is under mounting pressure. Competitors like On Holding, Hoka, New Balance, and even a resurgent Reebok are stealing share. Nike's turnaround under CEO Elliott Hill is in its early innings, but the timeline keeps getting pushed out, and there is no clear evidence yet that the bleeding has stopped.

This is not a broken company — Nike still has the world's most powerful athletic brand, $8.1B in cash, and 41% gross margins — but it is a deeply challenged one, and the stock, while cheaper than it's been in years, is not yet obviously cheap on fundamentals given the earnings trajectory.

## 2. Profitability & Growth Analysis

**The numbers are ugly, and the trend is negative.**

- **Revenue growth is essentially zero** over one year and the 3-year CAGR is slightly negative (-0.29%). For a company that once compounded revenue at 6-10% annually, this is a profound deceleration.
- **EPS has declined at a -16.77% CAGR over 3 years.** This isn't a blip — it's a structural deterioration.
- **Operating margin at 6.94%** is roughly half of Nike's historical ~12-14% range. This reflects markdowns to clear excess inventory, increased promotional activity, and loss of pricing power in key categories.
- **Net margin of 4.84%** — again, roughly half of normalized levels.
- **ROE of 16%** is being flattered by the aggressive share buybacks that have reduced the equity base. On an unlevered basis, returns on capital have deteriorated materially.

**Gross margin of 40.9%** is actually holding better than I'd expect, suggesting the brand still has some pricing power and the worst of the inventory markdown cycle may be behind Nike. This is the one bright spot in the P&L.

## 4. Competitive & Strategic Position

**The competitive landscape has shifted dramatically against Nike:**

- **On Holding** founders are buying shares hand over fist. The Swiss brand is gaining share in running and lifestyle at Nike's direct expense.
- **Hoka (Deckers)** continues to outperform in performance running.
- **New Balance** has executed a remarkable brand revival.
- **Reebok** is experiencing a cultural resurgence under Shaquille O'Neal's brand ambassadorship.
- **Adidas** has stabilized under Bjørn Gulden and is regaining ground.
- **Lululemon**, despite its own proxy fight drama, competes aggressively in athleisure.

Nike's core problem is strategic: the prior management team (under John Donahoe) over-rotated to DTC and digital at the expense of wholesale relationships, innovation, and cultural relevance. By the time Nike corrected course and brought back Elliott Hill (a Nike lifer), significant brand damage had been done, particularly among younger consumers. The pipeline of innovative product has been thin. The Jordan brand is experiencing oversaturation fatigue.

**China is a critical concern.** Per the WSJ report cited in the news, Nike's once-booming China market is under mounting pressure from domestic competitors (Li Ning, Anta) and geopolitical headwinds. The tariff environment adds another layer of risk to both sourcing costs and demand in this market.

## 10. Overall Verdict

**Nike is a HOLD at best, and patience will be required.**

I want to be direct: this is not a table-pounding buy, despite the "iconic brand at 12-year lows" narrative. The fundamentals are deteriorating, not improving. The P/FCF of 59x screams caution. The competitive position is the weakest it has been in decades. The turnaround is early, uncertain, and the timeline keeps slipping.

That said, I am not recommending an outright AVOID because: (1) the brand, while damaged, is not dead — Nike still does $46B+ in revenue and has global infrastructure that is essentially irreplaceable; (2) the CEO is buying stock in size; (3) gross margins are holding, suggesting the floor on profitability may be close; and (4) at some point, mean reversion in this industry is likely.

**For new money:** I would wait for evidence of stabilization — specifically, a quarter of sequential revenue growth and operating margin expansion — before initiating a position. The risk/reward at $42 is balanced, not asymmetrically favorable.

**For existing holders:** Hold, but set a stop-loss around $38. If the stock breaks below $40 on earnings, the bear case is in play.
